### Video 1 — brief

**Template:** A (biography)
**Real name/case:** Alice Augusta Ball (24 Jul 1892 – 31 Dec 1916), Black American chemist,
first woman and first Black person to earn a master's degree from the University of Hawaii
(1915).

**Why this one, one line:** She solved a medical problem doctors had failed at for centuries,
died at 24 before she could publish it, and the man who took her notes put his own name on the
cure and spent decades collecting the credit.

**Comparable videos:** No specific comparable videos watched this session — vidIQ account is
out of credits, so this isn't backed by watching an actual episode the way the template asks
for. What it's built on instead: `roadmap.md`'s finding that Template A (dark adjective +
life/tale + name) is the proven, high-ceiling pattern across Forgotten Lives, female_tales, and
Dynastic Profiles, and that stolen-credit / erasure framing is the thing separating working
channels from the two failed ones (plain, admiring titles). Once vidIQ credits are back, watch
one Forgotten Lives biography episode before Rishabh scripts video 2, to actually ground this
field the way it's supposed to work.

**Storyboard (beats, not script):**
1. Hook (0:00-0:30) — a disease so feared that Hawaii's government legally declared its
   patients dead and shipped them to a remote settlement. Then: a 23-year-old woman solved it
   in a lab, and you've never heard her name.
2. Who she was — a Black chemistry graduate student in 1915 Hawaii, working under Dr. Harry
   Hollmann at the Leprosy Investigation Station, at a time when neither her race nor her age
   should have put her anywhere near the era's biggest unsolved medical problem.
3. The dead end everyone else hit — chaulmoogra oil had been the only known treatment for
   Hansen's disease (leprosy) for centuries, but it was too thick to inject and caused patients
   to vomit it back up when taken orally. Effective in theory, useless in practice.
4. Her breakthrough — she chemically modified the oil's fatty acids into water-soluble ethyl
   esters, making it injectable and absorbable. It worked. She was 23. She hadn't published it yet.
5. The death — she fell seriously ill in 1916 and died within the year, at 24. Flag below:
   cause is disputed, do not state one as settled fact.
6. The theft — Arthur Dean, her research advisor and the university's dean (later its
   president), had access to her unpublished process. After her death he scaled it into mass
   production by 1919 and published it as the "Dean method." Her name appears nowhere in his
   papers on it. It stayed the standard leprosy treatment worldwide until antibiotics in the 1940s.
7. Close — Hollmann published a 1922 paper trying to correct the record, calling it the "Ball
   method" throughout and rejecting Dean's claim that his work was an improvement on hers. It
   didn't fix anything for decades. Real recognition didn't arrive until the 1990s, when a
   researcher found her mentioned in a 1932 book — and the University of Hawaii didn't
   formally honor her until 2000, 84 years after she died curing a disease that still carried
   her name by then, not his.

**Flags for Rishabh:**
- **Cause of death is disputed — do not state one as fact.** Some sources say likely chlorine
  gas exposure during a lab demonstration; older sources say tuberculosis; Wikipedia says
  simply "died unexpectedly" and declines to settle it. Script it as disputed/unknown, not as
  a specific cause.
- **The 1990s rediscovery detail** (a researcher finding her mentioned in the 1932 book *The
  Samaritans of Molokai*) needs a name and date check before it goes in — I have the shape of
  it, not a fully sourced version.
- **"Until antibiotics in the 1940s"** — confirm the exact drug/decade before stating it as a
  hard date; multiple sources agree on "until sulfone drugs arrived" but I haven't nailed the
  precise year.
- Everything else above traces to Wikipedia's Alice Ball entry, the National Women's History
  Museum biography, and a 2023 Conversation article — solid ground for a first draft, but
  Rishabh should still pull primary sourcing before recording, per the channel's own standard.
